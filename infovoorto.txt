Here is exactly why adding the Time-Window Flushing strategy unexpectedly sped up your program:

1. You Cured "Map Bloat" and Hash Collisions
Before this change, your Aggregator (shard.IPs) was a traditional Go map that just kept growing and growing endlessly until the program finished.

As a Go map gets massive, it experiences Hash Collisions (multiple IPs trying to occupy the same memory bucket).
To resolve this, Go has to pause and "re-hash" or grow the map, which takes time.
Why it's faster now: By constantly successfully flushing old IPs using delete(), you are keeping the internal Go map incredibly small and lean. When the map is small, inserting new IPs is mathematically much faster because there are zero hash collisions and zero resizing pauses.
2. The L3 CPU Cache Strikes Again!
Remember when we talked about why sending 2MB chunks was faster than 16MB chunks? The exact same hardware principle applies here to your memory maps!

A massive, multi-gigabyte map will not fit in your ultra-fast CPU L3 Cache. The CPU has to constantly reach out to the slow System RAM to find the stats for an IP address.
Why it's faster now: Because the map only holds a strict 10-minute trailing window of data, the entire statistical structure likely fits physically on the CPU die. The aggregator threads can lock, update, and unlock the shards blazingly fast because the map never leaves the processor's immediate vicinity.
3. Less Work for the Garbage Collector
When the Garbage Collector runs, it has to scan memory to see what is still alive and what is dead. Scanning a permanently living map with 200,000 deep statistical objects takes a long time and slows down the CPU.
When you flush and delete the data incrementally, the Garbage Collector can sweep up those small, dead chunks much more efficiently in the background without causing huge system-wide pauses.
